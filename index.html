From farm to cafe

Australia grows almost no coffee, but about half of us drink it every week. This page follows coffee from our cafes back to the farms that grow it, and on to the market that sets its price.

1. Coffee in Australia

Australia had 56,304 cafes and restaurants in June 2025, 616 more than a year before. But the population grew faster than the shops did, so there are now slightly fewer cafes per person than there were in 2024. Victoria is still the most cafe-dense state, with 25 for every 10,000 people — more than two and a half times the Northern Territory.

Small businesses

Nine in ten cafes and restaurants in Australia have fewer than 20 employees. In Victoria one in four has no employees at all, which means the owner does the work.

2. Where our coffee comes from

None of those cafes are served by an Australian farm. In 2025 Australia brought in 112 million kilograms of coffee from 22 coffee-growing countries, which is about four kilograms of beans for every person in the country. Five of those countries supply seven of every ten kilograms.

Our closest neighbour

Brazil supplies more than a quarter of the coffee we import from coffee-growing countries. Papua New Guinea, our nearest neighbour, is the fourth-biggest source.

3. At the source

Go one step further back and the picture narrows again. The world grew 10.6 million tonnes of coffee in 2024 across 76 countries, and Australia is not on that list at all. Brazil, Vietnam, Colombia, Indonesia and Ethiopia between them grow close to three quarters of it, so a bad season in any one of them is eventually felt in every cafe in section 1.

Yield

Yield is how much coffee a farm grows on each hectare of land. Vietnam gets about three tonnes per hectare, over four times as much as Ethiopia.

Changing places

Vietnam grew almost no coffee in 1990. It was 17th in the world. By 2000 it was second, and it has stayed there ever since. Today it grows about 22 times as much coffee as it did in 1990.

Mexico went the other way. It was the third-biggest grower in 1990 and has slipped to 11th.

4. The price of the bean

Between the farm and the cafe sits a commodity market. Wholesale prices fell about a fifth from their November 2025 high to September 2026, and Arabica beans kept costing roughly twice what Robusta did all the way through.

So the coffee in an Australian cup travels a long way and passes through a price nobody here sets. The businesses at the end of that chain are small ones: nine in ten have fewer than twenty staff, and a quarter of Victoria's have no staff at all. When the wholesale price moves, they are the ones who decide whether to wear it or pass it on.
